Renal tubular acidosis types 1, 2, 3 and 4: step-by-step mechanism

Normal function

How the four cells work when everything is fine.

  1. Na⁺ flows down the tubular lumen and enters each cell through its own channel

    Filtered Na⁺ travels along the lumen from left to right. In the proximal tubule it enters through NHE3 (1 Na⁺ in, 1 H⁺ or 1 NH₄⁺ out), in the thick ascending limb through NKCC2 (which waits until it has 1 Na⁺ and 1 K⁺ or NH₄⁺ to carry them together with 2 Cl⁻; the Cl⁻ exits through ClC-Kb) and in the collecting duct through ENaC. Whatever is not reabsorbed leaves in the urine.

  2. The proximal tubule recovers the filtered HCO₃⁻ without it crossing the membrane

    For every Na⁺ that enters through NHE3, one H⁺ leaves into the lumen. That H⁺ joins the filtered HCO₃⁻ (waiting in the lumen) and membrane carbonic anhydrase IV converts it into CO₂ + H₂O. The CO₂ enters the cell, carbonic anhydrase II turns it back into HCO₃⁻ and NBCe1 returns it to the blood (1 Na⁺ with 3 HCO₃⁻). This recovers 80% of the 4,000 mEq of HCO₃⁻ that are filtered each day.

  3. Aldosterone turns on ENaC through the MR receptor

    Renin from the kidney generates angiotensin II; this stimulates the adrenal gland, which releases aldosterone. Aldosterone enters the principal cell, binds the MR, the MR turns on SGK1 in the nucleus and SGK1 inactivates Nedd4-2, the enzyme that removes ENaC from the membrane. Result: more ENaC in the membrane.

  4. The Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase pumps Na⁺ out and K⁺ in; the lumen is left negative

    The pump works in cycles with a fixed count: it waits until it has 3 Na⁺ inside (those that entered through ENaC) and 2 K⁺ outside (from the blood); only then does it pump the 3 Na⁺ out and the 2 K⁺ in. Watch them accumulate until the count is complete. Since Na⁺ entered without an anion, the tubular lumen is left electrically negative (about −40 mV): the lumen meter is calculated from the Na⁺ that enter through ENaC.

  5. The negative voltage drives K⁺ through ROMK and H⁺ through the H⁺-ATPase

    The negative lumen attracts cations: K⁺ from the principal cell leaves through ROMK into the urine, and H⁺ from the neighboring type A intercalated cell leaves through the H⁺-ATPase. For every H⁺ secreted, carbonic anhydrase leaves behind a new HCO₃⁻; AE1 moves it into the blood only in exchange for a Cl⁻ that comes in (1 : 1 exchange, which is why it waits until it has both).

  6. Aldosterone and acidosis directly stimulate the H⁺ pump

    Besides the voltage, the H⁺-ATPase has two stimuli of its own: aldosterone (through a rapid pathway, within minutes, via protein kinase C) and systemic acidosis, which makes the cell insert into the membrane more pumps stored in vesicles. That is why in acidosis with a healthy kidney H⁺ secretion multiplies.

  7. The proximal tubule makes NH₄⁺ and the thick ascending limb recycles it

    Glutamine enters the proximal cell: glutaminase and PEPCK convert it into 2 NH₄⁺ (which leave into the lumen through NHE3) and 2 new HCO₃⁻ (which leave into the blood through NBCe1). In the thick ascending limb NH₄⁺ takes the K⁺ site on NKCC2 and passes into the medullary interstitium, where it accumulates as the NH₃ pool.

  8. In the collecting duct NH₃ + H⁺ → NH₄⁺: this is how the day's acid is excreted

    NH₃ from the pool enters the type A cell through Rhbg and leaves into the lumen through Rhcg. There it joins the secreted H⁺ and is trapped as NH₄⁺, which cannot diffuse back and leaves in the urine. Normal balance: the acid from the diet is excreted (urine pH 5–6), the K⁺ from the diet as well, and plasma HCO₃⁻ stays at 24.

  9. The H⁺ that finds no NH₃ binds phosphate: titratable acid

    The filtered phosphate that the proximal tubule did not reabsorb arrives as HPO₄²⁻ and waits in the lumen of the collecting duct. Every secreted H⁺ that meets it converts it into H₂PO₄⁻, which leaves in the urine. This is "titratable acid" (measured by titrating the urine to pH 7.4): about 20–30 mEq/day, a third of the acid excreted. Unlike NH₄⁺, it can hardly increase in acidosis because it depends on the phosphate that is filtered.

  10. The H⁺/K⁺-ATPase: another H⁺ into the lumen, a K⁺ back

    An apical pump of the type A cell that moves 1 H⁺ out and recovers 1 K⁺ from the lumen. With normal K⁺ it works little (orange circle); it is induced when K⁺ is low. That is why hypokalemia, on its own, increases H⁺ secretion (and ammoniagenesis) and tends toward alkalosis, and hyperkalemia does the opposite.

  11. The balance: net acid excretion = NH₄⁺ + titratable acid − urine HCO₃⁻

    The diet produces about 70 mEq of acid a day (1 mEq/kg). To keep HCO₃⁻ at 24 the kidney has to excrete the same amount: about 40 as NH₄⁺, about 30 as titratable acid and almost no HCO₃⁻ in the urine. Every H⁺ excreted is a new HCO₃⁻ that returns to the blood. In the urine box and in the laboratory strip, "net acid excretion" sums up that balance; in the blood, Cl⁻ rises as much as HCO₃⁻ falls when the acidosis is a non-anion gap acidosis.

Diarrheal acidosis · healthy kidney (control)

Secretory diarrhea, intestinal and pancreatic fistulas or drains, ureterosigmoidostomy, laxatives. It is the key comparison: the other major cause of non-anion gap acidosis, with a kidney that responds well.

  1. The gut loses HCO₃⁻ in the stool

    Intestinal and pancreatic secretions are rich in HCO₃⁻, and in diarrhea the colon exchanges it for Cl⁻. Between 30 and 100 mEq of HCO₃⁻ are lost each day in the stool (look at the arrow from the blood to the "STOOL" box). The urine is already beginning to acidify, but the kidney has not yet started its big response, the NH₄⁺ one.

  2. Cl⁻ rises as much as HCO₃⁻ falls: the anion gap does not change

    The Cl⁻ that the gut reabsorbs in exchange for HCO₃⁻ takes its place in the blood. Na⁺ minus (Cl⁻ + HCO₃⁻) stays at ≈ 12: hyperchloremic (normal anion gap) acidosis, the same as in the RTAs. The plasma anion gap does not distinguish the cause; you have to look at the urine.

  3. Acidosis turns on ammoniagenesis in the proximal tubule

    Within 2–5 days acidosis multiplies glutaminase, GDH, PEPCK and glutamine entry through SNAT3. Watch twice as much glutamine come in and twice as much NH₄⁺ leave into the lumen through NHE3 and twice as much new HCO₃⁻ into the blood through NBCe1. The hypokalemia that accompanies diarrhea stimulates it even more.

  4. The thick ascending limb carries more NH₄⁺ to the interstitium

    With more NH₄⁺ in the lumen and low K⁺, NKCC2 takes up NH₄⁺ instead of K⁺ more often and leaves it in the medullary interstitium through NHE4/KCC4: the NH₃ pool fills up.

  5. The type A cell inserts more H⁺ pumps

    Acidosis, and the aldosterone that rises because diarrhea removes volume, make the type A cell insert more H⁺-ATPases into the membrane. More H⁺ leaves into the lumen and for each one a new HCO₃⁻ is born that AE1 returns to the blood.

  6. Urine NH₄⁺ multiplies: negative urine anion gap

    With plenty of NH₃ available, almost every H⁺ is trapped as NH₄⁺: excretion rises from 40 to more than 100–200 mEq/day and the urine pH falls below 5.3. NH₄⁺ leaves accompanied by Cl⁻, so urine Cl⁻ exceeds Na⁺ + K⁺: a negative urine anion gap. This is the finding that separates it from all the RTAs (in which the gap is positive).

  7. K⁺ is lost and the H⁺/K⁺-ATPase is activated

    Diarrheal stool carries K⁺ and the high aldosterone increases renal secretion: hypokalemia. The H⁺/K⁺-ATPase of the type A cell is induced and recovers K⁺ from the lumen in exchange for more H⁺. Contrast with type 4 RTA: there the high K⁺ brakes ammoniagenesis; here the low K⁺ stimulates it.

  8. Result: diarrheal acidosis with a healthy kidney

    Low HCO₃⁻ and high Cl⁻ with a normal anion gap, low K⁺, urine pH < 5.3, high urine NH₄⁺ (> 40 mEq/L in a spot urine) and a negative urine anion gap; high net acid excretion. Careful: with severe hypokalemia and plenty of NH₃, the urine pH can rise above 5.5 without any RTA (there is buffer to spare, not a lack of H⁺); that is why you look at NH₄⁺, not just the pH. It is treated by replacing volume, K⁺ and bicarbonate.

Type 2 RTA · proximal

Fanconi syndrome (myeloma, cystinosis, tenofovir, ifosfamide), acetazolamide and topiramate, NBCe1 mutations (with ocular abnormalities).

  1. NBCe1 stops moving HCO₃⁻ into the blood

    The defect is in the basolateral exit: an NBCe1 mutation, the mitochondrial damage of Fanconi (without ATP the Na⁺/K⁺ pump does not maintain the gradient that drives NBCe1) or inhibition of carbonic anhydrase by acetazolamide. Na⁺ no longer couples to the transporter and the HCO₃⁻ made by carbonic anhydrase II is left waiting inside the cell: watch it pile up at NBCe1.

  2. The cell full of HCO₃⁻ brakes carbonic anhydrase II

    When no more HCO₃⁻ fits, carbonic anhydrase II cannot keep producing: the CO₂ that comes in bounces back. In the lumen, the H⁺ from NHE3 no longer has anywhere to take the filtered HCO₃⁻ and it is lost downstream. Proximal HCO₃⁻ reabsorption stops.

  3. The filtered HCO₃⁻ escapes along the lumen into the urine

    What the proximal tubule does not recover (15% or more of the filtered load) overwhelms the capacity of the loop and the collecting duct and leaves in the urine: bicarbonaturia, alkaline urine (pH > 7) and loss of the Na⁺ and K⁺ that accompany the HCO₃⁻. A high urine pH at this stage does not mean the type A cell is sick: it is simply receiving more HCO₃⁻ than it can titrate.

  4. Plasma HCO₃⁻ falls to a new threshold (≈ 15–18 mEq/L)

    HCO₃⁻ is lost until the filtered load falls below what the diseased tubule can still reabsorb. From that point on almost no HCO₃⁻ escapes: the acidosis is moderate and stabilizes on its own. That is why type 2 RTA is "self-limited" and why it is so hard to correct: any HCO₃⁻ given above the threshold is lost in the urine.

  5. The type A cell is healthy: the urine becomes acid again

    With little HCO₃⁻ reaching the collecting duct, the H⁺-ATPase and the NH₄⁺ trap work normally: the urine pH falls below 5.5 and NH₄⁺ excretion is normal. This is the key difference from distal RTA: the distal nephron acidifies the urine when it is given the chance. The diagnostic test works the other way around: on giving HCO₃⁻ and raising the plasma level above the threshold, the fractional excretion of HCO₃⁻ exceeds 15% and the urine pH rises.

  6. The loss of Na⁺ activates aldosterone and K⁺ is lost

    The Na⁺ and water that escaped with the HCO₃⁻ reduce volume: renin and aldosterone rise, ENaC works harder, the lumen becomes more negative and ROMK secretes more K⁺. Result: hypokalemia, which worsens with bicarbonate treatment (more HCO₃⁻ and Na⁺ reach the collecting duct). That is why it is corrected with K⁺ salts and, sometimes, thiazides.

  7. Result: type 2 RTA

    Moderate hyperchloremic acidosis (HCO₃⁻ 15–18) with a normal anion gap, hypokalemia, variable urine pH (high if plasma HCO₃⁻ is above the threshold; < 5.5 at steady state), FE of HCO₃⁻ > 15% after a bicarbonate load, preserved urine NH₄⁺. If it is part of a Fanconi syndrome: glycosuria, phosphaturia, aminoaciduria and uricosuria. Urine citrate is normal (the acidosis is mild), which is why there are almost never stones or nephrocalcinosis; in children, rickets and short stature.

Type 1 RTA · distal (classic)

Mutations of the H⁺-ATPase (ATP6V1B1, ATP6V0A4: with deafness) or of AE1 (SLC4A1); Sjögren syndrome, lupus, amphotericin B, lithium, hypercalciuria, medullary sponge kidney.

  1. The H⁺-ATPase of the type A cell stops pumping

    The pump is missing (ATP6V1B1/ATP6V0A4 mutation; in Sjögren the cell loses the pump from its membrane) or AE1 does not move the HCO₃⁻ out and the alkalinized cell stops producing H⁺ (SLC4A1 mutations). Amphotericin B does the same by another route: it opens pores in the membrane and the H⁺ already secreted leaks back. In every case the H⁺ does not reach the lumen: watch it bounce off the pump.

  2. Without secreted H⁺ no new HCO₃⁻ is born: AE1 is left waiting

    Carbonic anhydrase II only produces the H⁺ + HCO₃⁻ pair if the pump takes the H⁺ away. AE1 is left with Cl⁻ parked outside and no HCO₃⁻ to exchange; when no more Cl⁻ fits, it bounces back. The body's net gain of base (regenerating the HCO₃⁻ spent on the acid from the diet) is lost.

  3. The urine cannot be acidified: pH > 5.5 even with acidosis

    This is the diagnostic defect: with spontaneous acidemia or after an acid load (NH₄Cl, or furosemide with fludrocortisone), the urine pH never falls below 5.5. And since there is no H⁺ in the lumen, the NH₃ that leaves through Rhcg is not trapped as NH₄⁺: it can diffuse back into the interstitium, so very little reaches the urine. Low urine NH₄⁺ and a positive urine anion gap.

  4. The acid from the diet accumulates and plasma HCO₃⁻ falls unchecked

    Unlike type 2 RTA, here there is no rescue threshold: every day the 50–100 mEq of acid from the diet are retained and HCO₃⁻ can fall below 10 mEq/L. Hyperchloremic (normal anion gap) metabolic acidosis, sometimes severe.

  5. The Na⁺ that enters through ENaC is no longer matched by H⁺: K⁺ is lost

    The lumen-negative voltage is still intact (ENaC works) but it is no longer discharged by H⁺ secretion: the whole push falls on ROMK. Added to the secondary hyperaldosteronism from the salt loss, K⁺ goes into the urine: hypokalemia, sometimes severe (paralysis). The H⁺/K⁺-ATPase is induced and recovers some K⁺, but it is not enough. Correcting the acidosis with bicarbonate improves the kaliuresis.

  6. Bone buffers the acid: calcium and citrate go into the urine

    Chronic acidosis dissolves bone (hypercalciuria) and increases proximal citrate reabsorption (hypocitraturia). High calcium, low citrate and alkaline urine: the recipe for nephrocalcinosis and calcium phosphate stones. In children, rickets and growth retardation.

  7. Result: type 1 RTA

    Hyperchloremic (normal anion gap) acidosis, potentially severe (HCO₃⁻ < 10), hypokalemia, urine pH always > 5.5, low urine NH₄⁺ (positive urine anion gap), FE of HCO₃⁻ < 5%, nephrocalcinosis and stones. Incomplete form: the urine is not acidified but there is no systemic acidosis (it is discovered through stones). The hereditary forms due to ATP6V1B1 come with sensorineural deafness. It is treated with alkali (K⁺ citrate), which corrects the acidosis, the hypokalemia and the hypocitraturia.

Type 3 RTA · mixed (carbonic anhydrase II deficiency)

Mutation of the CA2 gene (Guibaud-Vainsel syndrome): osteopetrosis, cerebral calcifications. Today it is considered a combination of type 1 + type 2 RTA.

  1. Carbonic anhydrase II is missing in the proximal tubule

    The CO₂ that enters from the lumen is not converted into HCO₃⁻: it bounces back. NBCe1 is healthy but receives no HCO₃⁻ from the filtrate, and proximal HCO₃⁻ reabsorption is lost as in type 2 RTA (only the new HCO₃⁻ from glutamine is left).

  2. The filtered HCO₃⁻ escapes into the urine

    The same as in type 2 RTA: bicarbonaturia, alkaline urine and loss of Na⁺ and K⁺ with the HCO₃⁻, until plasma HCO₃⁻ falls below the threshold.

  3. It is missing in the type A cell too: there is no H⁺ to pump

    The same enzyme makes the H⁺ of the intercalated cell. Without it the H⁺-ATPase has no substrate, AE1 has no HCO₃⁻ and the urine is not acidified even when plasma HCO₃⁻ has already fallen below the proximal threshold. This is what is "mixed": recovery fails and regeneration fails.

  4. Mixed acidosis: the two defects add up

    HCO₃⁻ falls (12–16 mEq/L), the urine stays alkaline, urine NH₄⁺ is low and there is hypokalemia from the kaliuresis of the HCO₃⁻ and from the secondary hyperaldosteronism.

  5. Result: type 3 RTA

    Besides the kidney, carbonic anhydrase II works in the osteoclast (which needs H⁺ to resorb bone) and in the brain: osteopetrosis with fractures and cranial nerve compression, cerebral calcifications, intellectual disability and short stature. It is very rare (more frequent in the Maghreb and the Middle East) and is treated with alkali.

Type 4 RTA · hypoaldosteronism

Diabetic nephropathy (hyporeninism), NSAIDs, ACE inhibitors and ARBs, heparin, calcineurin inhibitors, Addison disease. The most common of all the RTAs.

  1. Aldosterone is missing: the MR is left without a stimulus

    Angiotensin II is the signal that makes the adrenal gland release aldosterone. Here that signal fails: watch how angiotensin II bounces off the adrenal gland, the aldosterone particles stop coming out and nothing reaches the mineralocorticoid receptor (MR) of the principal cell any more. The animation shows the case of an ARB, which blocks the AT1 receptor of the adrenal gland. The same happens with low renin (diabetic nephropathy, NSAIDs, calcineurin inhibitors), with ACE inhibitors (angiotensin II is not formed), with heparin (it atrophies the zona glomerulosa) and in Addison disease (the gland is destroyed). Mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (spironolactone, eplerenone, finerenone) leave the MR without a stimulus by another route: aldosterone does arrive, but finds the receptor occupied; that is shown under the “RTA 4 · MR antagonist” button.

  2. With the MR empty, SGK1 switches off and Nedd4-2 stays active

    When aldosterone occupies the MR, the receptor enters the nucleus and makes the cell produce SGK1, the enzyme that keeps Nedd4-2 restrained. Without aldosterone that chain switches off: watch how the MR and SGK1 turn grey, the arrows linking them fade and the Nedd4-2 label changes to “ACTIVE”.

  3. Nedd4-2 removes ENaC from the membrane: Na⁺ no longer enters

    Active Nedd4-2 tags ENaC so the cell removes and destroys it. Within hours the apical membrane is left almost without channels: watch how the Na⁺ coming down the lumen bounces off ENaC and goes on by towards the urine.

  4. With no Na⁺ entering, the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase stalls

    The pump only runs its cycle when it completes the count: 3 Na⁺ inside and 2 K⁺ outside. Since ENaC no longer lets Na⁺ in, the Na⁺ count is never completed: the K⁺ that arrive from the blood are left waiting at the pump, and when no more fit, they bounce back and accumulate in the blood.

  5. The lumen loses its negative charge and ROMK is left without a push

    The lumen-negative voltage was created by Na⁺ entering without an anion: the lumen meter is calculated from the Na⁺ that actually enter through ENaC versus those that go on by. Now almost all the Na⁺ goes on by, the lumen becomes almost neutral and the little K⁺ that enters the cell has no force to leave through ROMK: it goes back to the blood through Kir4.1. The Na⁺ that is not reabsorbed is lost in the urine.

  6. The type A cell loses the push to secrete H⁺

    The H⁺-ATPase is electrogenic: that same negative voltage helped it. Without it, some of the H⁺ bounce off the pump and go back to the blood (in hypoaldosteronism the direct stimulus of aldosterone on the pump is switched off as well: look at the "Aldo · acidosis" box). For every H⁺ that does not leave, no new HCO₃⁻ is born either: AE1 is left waiting with Cl⁻ parked outside and there is no exchange. The acid from the diet starts to be retained. Notice too that a few aldosterone particles reappear: the high K⁺ stimulates the adrenal gland directly, without going through angiotensin II. It is very little and not enough to reactivate the MR.

  7. The high K⁺ enters the proximal tubule and brakes glutaminase

    K⁺ enters the proximal cell in exchange for H⁺; the cytosol becomes alkaline and glutaminase and PEPCK lower their activity. Glutamine keeps arriving through SNAT3 but bounces off the mitochondrion without being processed: less NH₄⁺ and less new HCO₃⁻ are made. This is the central link of type 4 RTA: the acidosis is a consequence of the hyperkalemia.

  8. In the thick ascending limb K⁺ competes with NH₄⁺ for NKCC2

    NH₄⁺ and K⁺ use the same site on the cotransporter: NKCC2 has only one slot for K⁺ or NH₄⁺ per cycle. With high luminal K⁺ (look at how many K⁺ now arrive along the lumen) that slot is almost always taken by K⁺: NH₄⁺ bounces off and goes on by without building the medullary gradient. Less NH₄⁺ reaches the interstitium through NHE4/KCC4 and the NH₃ pool empties.

  9. Little NH₃ reaches the collecting duct: little NH₄⁺ is trapped

    With the medullary pool empty, Rhcg has little NH₃ to move into the lumen. The few H⁺ secreted lower the urine pH, but there is no buffer to turn them into NH₄⁺: net acid excretion falls and plasma HCO₃⁻ drops day by day. The little NH₃ that arrives is indeed trapped as NH₄⁺, because the urine is acidic: urine NH₄⁺ is low because little NH₃ arrives, not because it escapes.

  10. Result: type 4 RTA

    Hyperkalemia (K⁺ ≈ 5.5), hyperchloremic (normal anion gap) metabolic acidosis (HCO₃⁻ ≈ 19), low urine pH (< 5.5), low urine NH₄⁺ and a positive urine anion gap. The acidosis pulls more K⁺ out of the cells and closes the vicious circle. Unlike ENaC blockade, here the urine does acidify: the few H⁺ that are secreted find no NH₃ to buffer them and the urine pH falls below 5.5. With ARBs the hyperkalemia is usually milder than in Addison disease, because the healthy adrenal gland still responds a little to the high K⁺ (the aldosterone particles that reappear).

Type 4 RTA · aldosterone resistance

Spironolactone, eplerenone, finerenone; pseudohypoaldosteronism type 1 from a mutation of the MR. Aldosterone is high but cannot act.

  1. The drug occupies the MR

    Aldosterone keeps arriving (it even rises, because the kidney loses salt and renin increases), but it bounces off: the receptor is occupied by spironolactone. Nothing that the MR does is going to happen.

  2. Without the MR signal, Nedd4-2 removes ENaC from the membrane

    The MR no longer turns on SGK1; without SGK1, Nedd4-2 stays active and tags ENaC so the cell removes it. Within hours the apical membrane is left almost without ENaC: the Na⁺ that comes down the lumen no longer enters the principal cell.

  3. With no Na⁺ entering, the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase stalls

    The pump only runs its cycle when it completes the count: 3 Na⁺ inside and 2 K⁺ outside. Since ENaC no longer lets Na⁺ in, the Na⁺ count is never completed: the K⁺ that arrive from the blood are left waiting at the pump, and when no more fit, they bounce back and accumulate in the blood.

Type 4 RTA · ENaC blockade (voltage defect)

Amiloride, triamterene, trimethoprim, pentamidine; obstructive uropathy, sickle cell disease, lupus. Also called hyperkalemic distal RTA.

  1. ENaC is blocked with normal or high aldosterone

    The drug plugs the channel from the lumen (amiloride and triamterene always; trimethoprim and pentamidine at high doses) or tubular damage removes it (obstruction, sickle cell disease). Aldosterone arrives, the MR and SGK1 work, but Na⁺ bounces off the membrane. Renin and aldosterone rise because of the salt loss.

  2. The pump stalls and the lumen loses its negative charge

    With no Na⁺ entering, the Na⁺/K⁺ pump does not complete its count of 3 Na⁺ and the K⁺ pile up waiting in the blood. The lumen meter, which is calculated from the Na⁺ that enter through ENaC, becomes almost neutral: ROMK loses its push and K⁺ goes back through Kir4.1.

  3. Without voltage, the H⁺-ATPase cannot build the gradient: urine pH > 5.5

    This is the difference from hypoaldosteronism: aldosterone does stimulate the H⁺ pump directly, but without a negative lumen the pump cannot concentrate H⁺ a thousandfold. The urine pH stays above 5.5 ("voltage defect"), and for every H⁺ that does not leave, no new HCO₃⁻ is born either.

  4. The high K⁺ enters the proximal tubule and brakes glutaminase

    K⁺ enters the proximal cell in exchange for H⁺; the cytosol becomes alkaline and glutaminase and PEPCK lower their activity. Glutamine keeps arriving through SNAT3 but bounces off the mitochondrion without being processed: less NH₄⁺ and less new HCO₃⁻ are made. This is the central link of type 4 RTA: the acidosis is a consequence of the hyperkalemia.

  5. In the thick ascending limb K⁺ competes with NH₄⁺ for NKCC2

    NH₄⁺ and K⁺ use the same site on the cotransporter: NKCC2 has only one slot for K⁺ or NH₄⁺ per cycle. With high luminal K⁺ (look at how many K⁺ now arrive along the lumen) that slot is almost always taken by K⁺: NH₄⁺ bounces off and goes on by without building the medullary gradient. Less NH₄⁺ reaches the interstitium through NHE4/KCC4 and the NH₃ pool empties.

  6. Little NH₃ reaches the collecting duct: little NH₄⁺ is trapped

    With the medullary pool empty, Rhcg has little NH₃ to move into the lumen. The few H⁺ secreted lower the urine pH, but there is no buffer to turn them into NH₄⁺: net acid excretion falls and plasma HCO₃⁻ drops day by day.

  7. Result: type 4 RTA

    Hyperkalemia (K⁺ ≈ 6.2), hyperchloremic (normal anion gap) metabolic acidosis (HCO₃⁻ ≈ 17), a urine pH that does not fall below 5.5, low urine NH₄⁺ and a positive urine anion gap. The acidosis pulls more K⁺ out of the cells and closes the vicious circle. With ENaC blockade the urine pH stays high (> 5.5) unlike in hypoaldosteronism; it is the finding that distinguishes them in the clinic.

Channels, pumps and transporters

What each part of the diagram does, what turns it on and what turns it off. Numbers in brackets link to the references.

ENaC — epithelial sodium channel (apical)

Lets Na⁺ in from the lumen. It is the rate-limiting step for Na⁺ transport in the collecting duct. Aldosterone, via MR → SGK1, phosphorylates Nedd4-2 and so prevents ENaC from being retrieved from the membrane (hours); over the longer term it increases transcription of the α subunit (hours to days). Angiotensin II also stimulates it directly. Amiloride, triamterene, trimethoprim and pentamidine block it from the lumen. Because Na⁺ enters without an anion, the lumen is left electrically negative: that voltage is what drives the secretion of K⁺ (ROMK) and of H⁺ (the neighboring type A cell). [12] [14] [15] [27] [34]

ROMK (Kir1.1) — apical potassium channel

Secretes K⁺ into the lumen. Two things drive it: the high intracellular K⁺ (maintained by the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase) and the lumen-negative voltage that ENaC generates. Aldosterone increases its number over days; high plasma K⁺ also induces it through a renal pathway of its own, independent of aldosterone. If ENaC is switched off, ROMK stays intact but with no electrical driving force: K⁺ secretion falls all the same. [16] [18] [34] [35]

BK (maxi-K) — flow-activated potassium channel

Second apical K⁺ channel. It opens with high tubular flow and with intracellular Ca²⁺. It explains why loop diuretics and a salt load increase K⁺ secretion even when aldosterone does not change. In the model it contributes 20% of K⁺ secretion and does not depend on the lumen-negative voltage that ENaC generates (it does depend on membrane voltage and on Ca²⁺, which are its own activators), which is why with amiloride K⁺ rises a lot but not without limit. [16] [20] [35]

Aquaporin-2 — water channel (apical)

Vasopressin (ADH) inserts it via the V2 receptor and cAMP. It reabsorbs water down the medullary osmotic gradient. It plays no part in type 4 RTA; it is drawn to complete the cell. Not modeled in version 1. [35] [36]

Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase — basolateral pump

It pumps 3 Na⁺ out to the blood and 2 K⁺ into the cell for each ATP. It keeps intracellular Na⁺ low (so that ENaC still has a gradient) and intracellular K⁺ high (so that ROMK has something to secrete). Aldosterone increases the α subunit over days; the entry of Na⁺ through ENaC activates it immediately. Calcineurin inhibitors reduce it. [13] [15] [17] [35]

Kir4.1 / Kir5.1 — basolateral potassium channel

It recycles K⁺ to the interstitium and sets the basolateral potential. It is the K⁺ "sensor" of the distal tubule: high plasma K⁺ depolarizes it and, via WNK/SPAK, switches off NCC in the distal tubule so that more Na⁺ reaches the collecting duct and more K⁺ is secreted. Not modeled in version 1. [12] [20] [35]

MR — mineralocorticoid receptor

Cytosolic receptor for aldosterone. Bound to it, it travels to the nucleus and switches on the transcription of SGK1 (30–60 min), αENaC, Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase and ROMK. Cortisol also binds the MR, but the enzyme 11β-HSD2 inactivates it in these cells; that is why the receptor "sees only" aldosterone. Spironolactone and eplerenone block it: aldosterone rises in the blood but does not act. [12] [14] [34]

SGK1 — aldosterone-induced kinase

It is the first gene the MR switches on. SGK1 phosphorylates Nedd4-2 and inactivates it; it also phosphorylates subunits of ENaC and of the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase. It is the fast link (hours) between the hormone and the channel. [13] [14] [15] [35]

Nedd4-2 — ubiquitin ligase

It tags ENaC with ubiquitin so that the cell retrieves it from the membrane and degrades it. When SGK1 phosphorylates it, Nedd4-2 stays inactive and ENaC remains at the surface. Without aldosterone (or with the MR blocked) Nedd4-2 becomes active again and ENaC disappears from the membrane within a few hours. [14] [15] [35]

Nucleus — genomic effects

The MR with aldosterone acts as a transcription factor. Early response (SGK1, 30–60 min) and late response (αENaC, the α subunit of the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase, ROMK; 6 h to days). That is why the hyperkalemia of type 4 RTA takes days to set in and to correct. [12] [13] [35]

Paracellular pathway — Cl⁻ follows Na⁺

Between cell and cell (tight junction) Cl⁻ passes from the lumen to the blood, driven by the same lumen-negative voltage that ENaC generates. It is the companion that makes NaCl reabsorption electrically neutral. When ENaC falls, paracellular Cl⁻ falls too and more NaCl is left in the urine (mild natriuresis). [17] [21] [35]

Vacuolar H⁺-ATPase — proton pump (apical)

An electrogenic pump that extrudes H⁺ into the lumen using ATP. It is the main mechanism of distal acidification. It is activated by systemic acidosis (which inserts more pumps into the membrane), by angiotensin II and by aldosterone (a genomic pathway through the MR and a rapid non-genomic pathway through PKC, within minutes). The lumen-negative voltage generated by ENaC in the neighboring principal cell makes its job easier; without that voltage (amiloride) the pump cannot achieve the gradient and the urine pH does not fall below 5.5. [9] [10] [11] [2] [34] [35]

H⁺/K⁺-ATPase — apical pump

It secretes H⁺ and reabsorbs K⁺ in exchange. It is induced when K⁺ is lacking (hypokalemia): that is why K⁺ depletion produces alkalosis. In hyperkalemia it has little role. In the model it is a small, constant flux. [9] [21] [35]

Rhcg / Rhbg — ammonia transporters

Rhesus glycoproteins that transport mainly NH₃ across the membranes of the intercalated cell: Rhbg/Rhcg on the basolateral side take up the NH₃ from the medullary interstitium and Rhcg on the apical side passes it into the lumen, where it binds the secreted H⁺ and is trapped as NH₄⁺ (it cannot diffuse back). Under an acid load, without Rhcg NH₄⁺ excretion falls by about half. What limits things here is not the transporter but how much NH₃ there is in the interstitium, and that depends on the proximal tubule and the thick ascending limb. [5] [7] [8] [34] [35]

AE1 (band 3, SLC4A1) — basolateral Cl⁻/HCO₃⁻ exchanger

It delivers to the blood the "new" HCO₃⁻ that carbonic anhydrase produces for each H⁺ secreted, in exchange for a Cl⁻ that enters. It is the body's net gain of base: every H⁺ that ends up in the urine (as NH₄⁺ or titratable acid) is a HCO₃⁻ that returns to the blood. Mutations of AE1 cause type 1 distal RTA. [9] [20] [35]

Basolateral chloride channel (ClC-Kb / barttin)

It lets out to the blood the Cl⁻ that entered through AE1 (type A cell) or through NKCC2 (thick ascending limb). It closes the chloride circuit. Mutations of ClC-Kb cause type 3 Bartter syndrome; mutations in its obligatory subunit, barttin, cause type 4, with sensorineural deafness. [20] [35]

Titratable acid — phosphate as a buffer

The filtered HPO₄²⁻ that reaches the collecting duct takes up a H⁺ and leaves as H₂PO₄⁻ (pKa 6.8). This is the "titratable acid": some 20–30 mEq/day, one third of net acid excretion (NAE = NH₄⁺ + titratable acid − urinary HCO₃⁻). It can hardly increase in acidosis, because it depends on the phosphate that is filtered; the adaptive response is provided by NH₄⁺. In CKD it falls along with the GFR. [4] [23] [25] [34]

Direct stimuli of the H⁺-ATPase: aldosterone and acidosis

Aldosterone activates the H⁺ pump of the type A cell within minutes through a non-genomic pathway (protein kinase C, Ca²⁺), in part independent of the MR, and over the long term increases its subunits. Systemic acidosis makes the cell insert into the apical membrane pumps stored in vesicles. Angiotensin II also stimulates it. In hypoaldosteronism this stimulus is lost in addition to the voltage. [9] [10] [11] [34] [35]

Intestinal loss of HCO₃⁻

The secretions of the small intestine, the pancreas and the bile are rich in HCO₃⁻, and the colon exchanges it for Cl⁻. In diarrhea 30–100 mEq/day are lost: hyperchloremic acidosis with a normal anion gap and hypokalemia. The healthy kidney responds by multiplying NH₄⁺ excretion: a negative urine anion gap, unlike the RTAs. [25] [23] [24] [34]

Carbonic anhydrase IV — the one in the lumen of the proximal tubule

An enzyme anchored to the apical membrane, facing the lumen. The H⁺ that NHE3 secretes binds the filtered HCO₃⁻ and forms H₂CO₃; CA IV dehydrates it instantly into CO₂ + H₂O, which enter the cell by diffusion. Without it the reaction would be hundreds of times slower and the HCO₃⁻ would pass on by. Acetazolamide inhibits it (along with the CA II of the cytoplasm): pharmacological proximal RTA. [4] [37] [36]

Carbonic anhydrase II of the proximal tubule

Inside the cell it rehydrates the CO₂ that entered from the lumen and turns it back into HCO₃⁻ (which leaves through NBCe1) + H⁺ (which goes back out through NHE3). It is the same cycle as in the type A cell, but here its job is to recover the filtered HCO₃⁻ (some 4,000 mEq/day, 80% in this segment), not to make new HCO₃⁻. In the lumen carbonic anhydrase IV helps it (H⁺ + HCO₃⁻ → CO₂ + H₂O). Its congenital deficiency causes type 3 RTA (mixed: proximal + distal) with osteopetrosis; acetazolamide inhibits it. [4] [37] [9] [34] [35]

Carbonic anhydrase II — factory of H⁺ and HCO₃⁻

It converts CO₂ + H₂O into H⁺ + HCO₃⁻ inside the cell. The H⁺ is pumped into the lumen and the HCO₃⁻ leaves to the blood: an inseparable pair. Its congenital deficiency causes type 3 RTA (mixed). Acetazolamide inhibits it. [4] [9] [34] [35]

Ammoniagenesis — glutaminase (PDG), GDH and PEPCK

Glutamine enters the mitochondrion: phosphate-dependent glutaminase releases the first NH₄⁺ and glutamate dehydrogenase the second. The remaining α-ketoglutarate is metabolized (PEPCK → gluconeogenesis) and yields 2 new HCO₃⁻. Acidosis increases these enzymes several-fold over 2–3 days. Hyperkalemia slows them: K⁺ enters the cell in exchange for H⁺, the cytosol becomes alkaline and the pathway shuts down. This is the central link of type 4 RTA: the acidosis is a consequence of the hyperkalemia. [3] [5] [6] [8] [26] [34] [35]

NHE3 — apical Na⁺/H⁺ exchanger

It reabsorbs Na⁺ in exchange for H⁺ (this is how 80% of the filtered HCO₃⁻ is reabsorbed). NH₄⁺ can occupy the H⁺ site: it is the main route of ammonium secretion into the tubule. Angiotensin II stimulates it. When it is inhibited or downregulated (Fanconi syndrome, acetazolamide) it contributes to proximal HCO₃⁻ wasting; in humans its mutations do not cause proximal RTA but congenital sodium diarrhea. [5] [6] [17] [35]

NBCe1 — basolateral Na⁺·3HCO₃⁻ cotransporter

It delivers to the blood the reabsorbed HCO₃⁻ and the new HCO₃⁻ from ammoniagenesis. Its mutations cause type 2 RTA with ocular involvement. [4] [35]

SNAT3 (SLC38A3) — glutamine entry

Basolateral glutamine transporter. Acidosis increases its expression to feed ammoniagenesis. In hyperkalemia less glutamine enters the pathway. [5] [8] [35]

NKCC2 — Na⁺·K⁺·2Cl⁻ cotransporter (apical, thick ascending limb)

It reabsorbs Na⁺, K⁺ and 2 Cl⁻. NH₄⁺ has the same hydrated size as K⁺ and occupies its site: this is how most of the NH₄⁺ coming from the proximal tubule is reabsorbed and accumulates in the medullary interstitium (the gradient that then feeds the collecting duct via Rhcg). When luminal K⁺ is high it competes with NH₄⁺ and less ammonium is recycled: the second blow of hyperkalemia to acid excretion. Furosemide blocks it. [3] [5] [7] [35]

ROMK in the thick ascending limb — recycles K⁺ into the lumen

It returns to the lumen the K⁺ that entered through NKCC2 so that the cotransporter is never left without luminal K⁺, and it generates the lumen-positive voltage that drives the paracellular reabsorption of Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺. Mutations: type 2 Bartter syndrome. [16] [20] [35]

Basolateral exit of NH₄⁺ (NHE4, KCC4, Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase)

The reabsorbed NH₄⁺ leaves to the medullary interstitium by several routes (NHE4, KCC4 and the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase itself, which accepts it in place of K⁺). There it is concentrated and, as NH₃, diffuses toward the medullary collecting duct. The medullary pool is the "tank" that final acid excretion lives off. [5] [7] [34] [35]

Renin — juxtaglomerular apparatus

It is released in response to low perfusion, little NaCl delivery to the macula densa and sympathetic stimulation; prostaglandins are necessary for its secretion. NSAIDs (no prostaglandins), diabetic nephropathy (damage to the juxtaglomerular apparatus, hyporeninemic hypoaldosteronism) and calcineurin inhibitors reduce it. It rises when aldosterone falls or salt is lost (Addison, spironolactone, amiloride). [31] [32] [29] [35]

Angiotensin II

It stimulates the synthesis of aldosterone in the zona glomerulosa and, by itself, ENaC, the H⁺-ATPase and proximal ammoniagenesis. ACE inhibitors prevent its formation and ARBs block its AT1 receptor: in both cases aldosterone falls even though renin rises. [1] [12] [9] [35]

Zona glomerulosa — aldosterone synthesis

Aldosterone is synthesized under two independent stimuli: angiotensin II and plasma K⁺ (high K⁺ depolarizes the glomerulosa cell and activates aldosterone synthase). Addison destroys the gland (aldosterone ≈ 0 with very high renin). Heparin reduces the number and the affinity of the AT1 receptors in the zona glomerulosa and atrophies it (days). In diabetic nephropathy the gland also responds poorly to K⁺. [30] [32] [19] [36]

Transepithelial lumen voltage

The entry of Na⁺ through ENaC without an accompanying anion leaves the lumen negative with respect to the blood (about −10 to −50 mV depending on the aldosterone status). That voltage drives cations toward the lumen: K⁺ through ROMK and H⁺ through the H⁺-ATPase of the type A intercalated cell. When ENaC falls, the voltage is lost and both secretions fall at the same time: that is the connection that turns a sodium problem into a potassium and acid problem. If the block is directly on ENaC (amiloride) the H⁺ pump is left without help and the urine pH cannot fall below 5.5. [2] [9] [27] [34]
